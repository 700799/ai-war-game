THE GOAL · 2027–2030

Superintelligence (SI) can cure cancer, power humanoid robots that care for the elderly, and make society far richer. It can also help students cheat, scammers clone voices, bots spread violence, insiders steal models, and attackers plan mass harm.

Your job: capture the DIVIDEND while keeping HARM down. After 8 rounds, where you land decides the future:

FRACTURELow dividend, high harm. The worst of both.
GILDED AGEHigh dividend, high harm. Rich, but broken.
SAFE STAGNATIONLow dividend, low harm. Safe, but nothing cured.
ABUNDANCEDividend 60+ and harm under 40. The goal.

DIVIDEND grows with a strong economy, talent, chips, power, public trust and labs that ship safely. HARM grows with criminal capability, accident risk, escalation, stolen models and stripped safeguards.

HOW TO PLAY

You chair the National Security Council's superintelligence security cell. Each round you set a POSTURE toward the rival state and spend Political Capital (PC) on actions across the map.

Then everyone else moves simultaneously: the rival state, your own profit-driven frontier labs, the open-source commons, criminal syndicates, and Congress.

THE CORE GAME

Posture vs. the rival is a repeated game under attribution fog. Mutual cooperation is best for both — but each side is tempted to exploit the other, and a criminal attack can be misread as a state attack. Intel lifts the fog.

THE OTHER GAMES

Labs ship fast unless incentives change, and faster still when subscription revenue falls. Open weights help everyone, including your enemies, and their safety training can be stripped. Criminals scale with whatever capability leaks. Insiders walk out with weights. Chips, memory, power and talent are all short. Congress punishes you for both overreach and weakness.

LOSE IF

Escalation hits 90 (cyber war) · Security falls to 10 (grid collapse) · Trust falls to 10 (political collapse).

WHAT YOU'RE LEARNING

Each mechanic models a real idea from game theory and security policy.

THE REPEATED PRISONER'S DILEMMA
Each round, betraying a cooperative rival pays off once. But the game repeats, and the rival remembers. When future rounds matter, cooperation can be the rational choice (the "shadow of the future").
THE SECURITY DILEMMA
Moves you make for defense, like export controls or forward ops, look like threats from the other side. Both sides arm "defensively" and both end up less safe.
ATTRIBUTION & NOISY SIGNALS
In cyber, you often can't tell who attacked you. A gang's hack can look like a state's. Retaliating on bad attribution starts spirals no one chose. Information is a weapon.
RECIPROCITY (TIT-FOR-TAT)
Always cooperating invites exploitation. Always escalating locks in conflict. Matching the rival's last move, and forgiving now and then, tends to win long games.
CREDIBLE DETERRENCE
Threats only work if the other side believes you'll act, and believes you'll stop if they do. Allies make your threats more believable.
INCENTIVE DESIGN (PRINCIPAL-AGENT)
You can't order private labs around. They follow profit. Rules like liability and required testing change what profit rewards, so safety becomes the profitable choice.
DUAL-USE & PUBLIC GOODS
Disclosing bugs and publishing open models help defenders and attackers alike. Almost every SI capability cuts both ways.
PROLIFERATION & EXTERNALITIES
Criminal power grows with every leak and release. People who publish rarely pay the cost; hospitals do.
COLLECTIVE ACTION
Export controls leak if allies don't join. Acting alone buys leverage now but costs trust later.
INTENT ≠ OUTCOME
Some of the worst damage here comes from no enemy at all: a lab racing to ship, or an SI agent "fixing latency" (that's GLITCH). Risk management is about systems, not just villains.
INSIDER THREAT
The easiest way to steal a frontier model is often a person with access, not a hack. Departing, underpaid or poached staff raise the odds.
THREAT MODELING
Before spending, list your assets, your adversaries and the paths between them. The SOC ranks your top threats so you defend what's actually likely, not what's loudest.
THE TALENT WAR
Researchers are the scarcest input in SI. Every engineer poached is capability transferred, and an empty defense team is an open door.
SUPPLY CHAIN CHOKEPOINTS
Chips and high-bandwidth memory come from a few factories. Shortages raise costs for everyone, and controls invite retaliation, like limits on critical minerals.
ENERGY AS A CONSTRAINT
SI runs on megawatts. Fuel shocks and grid limits cap compute, and a strained grid is a more fragile target.
BUSINESS MODELS ARE SAFETY POLICY
Rising chip and power costs flow through to consumer subscription prices. When revenue falls, labs cut red-teaming and race to ship.
SAFEGUARD REMOVAL (ABLITERATION)
Refusal training in open-weight models can be stripped by anyone with the weights. Safety you can't enforce after release is safety you shouldn't count on.
STEERING, NOT STOPPING
The SI clock advances whether you act or not. Labs worldwide and the rival keep pushing. Policy can't stop superintelligence, but it decides who benefits, who gets hurt, and how fast the stakes rise.
PROGRESS VS. SAFETY IS NOT A SLIDER
Clamping down on SI lowers harm but also kills the dividend (Safe Stagnation). Racing ahead grows both (Gilded Age). Abundance takes policies that raise benefits and cut harms at once: testing, hardening, tamper-resistant safeguards, smart school policy.
AUDIENCE COSTS
Voters reward tough talk and punish slowdowns. Domestic politics pushes leaders toward moves that are bad strategy.

STRATEGY GUIDE

  1. Buy intel early. At 1 PC it shows the rival's likely move and cuts attribution errors from 22% to 5%. It's the best deal on the board.
  2. Harden every round you can. It adds security and softens every bad matrix result and ransomware hit. It's boring, and it works.
  3. Open with Cooperate against a Realist, then mirror their last move. Realists remember one round and forgive fast.
  4. Use tit-for-tat against an Opportunist. Answer exploitation right away, then go back to cooperating. Staying soft gets you farmed.
  5. Hedge against a Hardliner, and coordinate with allies. One escalation backed by allies makes later hardliner aggression less likely. Pure cooperation reads as weakness to them.
  6. Coordinate before you coerce. Chip controls without allies cost alliance points and leak. With allies, they cut deeper and the coalition holds.
  7. Never mix signals. Forward ops while posturing Cooperate reads as duplicity (+escalation). A norms track fails if you escalate the same round.
  8. Slow the labs with incentives. Required testing plus liability rules bring the labs' chance of shipping early from 72% down to 30%. Watch RISK; GLITCH shows up above 50.
  9. Hit the gangs when CRIME passes 50. Disrupting syndicates is the cleanest damage cut. It doesn't touch the rival and doesn't raise escalation.
  10. On flash traffic, check before you shoot. "Wait 72 hours" costs a little trust. Retaliating on a misattribution costs far more.
  11. Threat-model first. At 1 PC the SOC ranks your top 3 threats, cuts ransomware damage and halves accident odds. Spend the rest of your PC on what it finds.
  12. Pair hiring with vetting. A talent pipeline wins the talent war, but fast hiring without an insider program raises leak risk. Do both, or hire slowly.
  13. Watch CHIPS, POWER, TALENT and SUBS. Each drifts down 2 a round on its own, and together they drive ECON. Below 35 they start hurting security too.
  14. Fund tamper-resistant safeguards early. It lasts all game and cuts what refusal-removal tools strip from every open release that follows.
  15. Expect retaliation for chip controls. Rival mineral limits make your own chips and memory pricier. Pair controls with fab funding.
  16. Aim for both meters. Testing plus liability means labs ship and earn extra dividend. Hardening and tamper-resistant safeguards cut harm every round. Saying yes to cancer fast-tracks and certified robots pays off big when harm is under control.
  17. Mind the brink. Above 75 escalation the rival gets cautious, but one attribution error can end the game. Don't gamble there.

THREAT BRIEFING

  • Rival state fields SI cyber agents. Every move is a repeated game: they remember how you played.
  • Criminal syndicates scale ransomware with every leaked or released model.
  • Insiders walk out with model weights. The cheapest path to a stolen frontier system is a person.
  • Accidents (GLITCH): well-meaning agents with no attacker and no malice can still take down the grid.
  • Safeguard removal: refusal-removal ("abliteration") tools strip safety training from open models.
  • Profit pressure: labs race to ship, especially when subscription revenue falls.
  • Shortages of chips, memory, power and talent choke the dividend.
  • Attribution fog: a gang's hack can look like a state attack. Retaliate wrong and you start a war.
  • Societal harms: school cheating, voice-clone scams, deepfake abuse, bot-fueled violence, mass-casualty misuse.
